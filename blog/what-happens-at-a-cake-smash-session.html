What actually happens at a cake smash session

By Deep Arora · Still & Golden Photography · Frankston & South-East Melbourne

Most parents booking a first birthday shoot have seen the end result — a one-year-old sitting in the wreckage of a cake, grinning, icing in their hair — without ever seeing how you get there. So here's the whole hour, start to finish, including the bits nobody puts on Instagram.

It happens in your home

There's no studio to drive to. I bring one to you: a backdrop, the stand, balloons, a birthday banner, and the lighting to make it all work. It sets up in a corner of your lounge room in about twenty minutes, and packs down just as fast.

Think of it as a travelling studio. You get the clean, colourful backdrop that makes cake smash photos pop, without packing a nappy bag, wrangling a one-year-old into the car, and arriving somewhere unfamiliar right before asking them to perform.

That familiarity matters more than people expect. A one-year-old who is already at home, near their own toys, with their own parents in the room, is a one-year-old far more likely to actually enjoy themselves.

The hour, in order

Every session follows roughly the same arc, and it's worth knowing because the interesting part isn't the smash — it's the build-up.

First, the clean photos. We start before any cake appears: your one-year-old in their outfit, a few frames on their own, then the family together. Ten minutes, while everyone is still spotless. This is where the framed photo usually comes from.

Then the cake goes down. And almost nobody dives straight in. What actually happens is a long, suspicious pause — a look at the cake, a look at you, one finger extended to test whether this is genuinely allowed.

That hesitation is my favourite part of the whole session. It's the last moment before they work out they're allowed to destroy it.

Then it turns. Usually within a minute of the first taste. Both hands go in, the cake stops being an object and becomes a material, and there's a stretch of five or ten minutes that is pure, uncomplicated delight. That's the part you booked for.

And then it's done. One-year-olds finish abruptly. When the interest goes, it goes — and that's fine, because by then we have the whole story.

About the cake

You provide the cake. That's deliberate — you know your child's allergies and what they're allowed to eat, and I'd rather you had complete control over what goes in front of them than have me guess.

A few things genuinely help the photos:

  • Buttercream, not fondant. Fondant is firm and gives under small hands about as well as a frisbee. Buttercream collapses properly.
  • One tier is plenty. A smash cake doesn't need to be big or expensive. Small cakes photograph better next to a one-year-old, and less of it ends up wasted.
  • Light icing over dark. Pale colours keep the photos bright and don't stain skin, clothes or your carpet. Deep red and black are the ones to avoid.
  • Bring it out cold. A cake straight from the fridge holds its shape for the clean shots before it starts sliding.

Once you book, I send through a short guide with cake and outfit suggestions, so none of this has to be worked out from memory. There's also a fuller walk-through in how to choose a smash cake.

"What if my baby just cries?"

This is the question I'm asked most, and it deserves a straight answer: sometimes they do. A decent number of one-year-olds take one look at the cake and want absolutely nothing to do with it. Some object to the icing on their hands. Some are simply having a day.

None of that ruins the session. We stop, you have a cuddle, we try a snack that isn't cake, and quite often they come back around five minutes later on their own terms. If they don't, we've still got the clean portraits and the family photos from the start of the hour — which is exactly why we do those first.

As a dad of two, I've seen every version of this, and I've never once left a session without a gallery. The hesitant ones usually come good. The criers, honestly, tend to produce the funniest set of photos in the end.

When to book

Most families book their cake smash around four to six weeks before the birthday. That's usually enough notice to get the date you want, and late enough that you know how your little one is travelling.

The session itself doesn't have to land on the birthday. Any time in the few weeks either side works — and a session a fortnight beforehand has the advantage of giving you the photos in hand before the party.
